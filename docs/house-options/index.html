Kettle Van

critic rank 10 of 10

A little round-shouldered camper parked for the night under a streetlamp, one amber window lit, kettle steam on the roof.

The feeling of walking past someone else's warm window on a cold street and wanting to be let in.

evening
Evening. The sliding door is shut - a dusty violet recessed panel, centred, running to the bottom edge, with a thin honey seam of light escaping along its foot where it meets the ground. The big living-area window on the right is full amber; you can read a kettle shape and a mug against it as dark cut-outs, no more. The streetlamp is on and drops a cool pale pool across the roof above the door. A soft plume of steam curls up off the roof flue. Oat upper panel still holds a little daylight memory, teal lower panel already going blue.
open
The same van, the side door slid fully back into the body so the doorway becomes a clean amber rectangle from y-mid to the bottom edge - honey at the centre, cooler amber at the edges, with the dark frame of the door aperture keeping a hard outline round it. Warm light lands on the doorsill and up the inside of the body so the opening looks deep rather than painted on. The window stays lit. Steam still rising. The cool roof pool is unchanged, which is the whole point: cold above, warm below.
night
The hero, and the state everything else was built to serve. Inside, the lamp is down to a low ember: the window drops from full amber to a dim honey slot, barely brighter than the body. The streetlamp now does all the work - the cool pale pool on the roof is the brightest thing in the sprite, spilling down over the oat panel and rimming the top of the doorframe in blue-white. The teal lower body goes slate, the violet shadows deepen, the whole van cools by two steps. Steam thins to a wisp because the kettle is off. The door is shut and only a single faint warm line survives at its foot - the last light in the picture, and it is at the pet's feet. Cool exterior, one dying warm ember: that is the image.
the cast
Mostly frames them, with one honest conflict. At standing height - the lower two thirds, which is all the pet's body covers - the van is muted teal and dusty violet: cool, flat, unpatterned, exactly the quiet backdrop a red cap, a blue gingham scarf and rust dungarees want. Red cap against muted teal is near-complementary and will pop hard. The boy's green-yellow stripes are the weakest pairing against teal, but he stays brighter in value so he still separates. Two real problems: the oat upper panel is close in value to the cream-and-apricot puppy, which is why it is confined above y 74, over the pet's head and not behind it; and Hanu in orange and gold standing in a warm amber doorway will lose contrast in the open state, which is what the dark doorframe edge and the cooler amber at the aperture's edges are there to fix. The building is cooler and quieter than the cast on purpose - it never competes, it just makes them look warm.
risk
The streetlamp is the thing that breaks this. Its mast and arm are the only thin shapes in the design, and thin shapes are exactly what the white-backdrop flood key punishes: if the arm is drawn even slightly delicate, the backdrop leaks through it, and if the generator separates the lamp head from the arm by a pixel the pipeline counts it as a second figure and fails the frame count. The mitigation is that the mast's foot is hidden behind the van's nose so the lamp is one connected silhouette with the vehicle, and the arm is specified chunky - but chunky enough to survive the key may also look like a club rather than a streetlamp. The steam plume carries the same risk and is the first thing to cut if the outline cannot hold it. Second, smaller risk: a low horizontal van whose doorway must reach the bottom edge has to give up the daylight-under-the-chassis that makes a van read as a van, so at 120px it can flatten into a bus or a caravan - and a blocky mock genuinely cannot prove which way that goes, because the rescue is the nose curvature and the wheel arches, which are detail the mock cannot show.

the cast
It frames them. Every exterior value sits in the 30-45% band and every exterior hue is indigo, violet or slate, so a cream-and-apricot puppy or Hanu in orange and gold pops forward without any outlining trick. Two honest watch-outs. First, the blue gingham scarf against the street-lit wall (#57628A) is the one place a character colour and a building colour share a hue family; it survives because the scarf is saturated and the wall is not, but if the scarf is pale it will mush - which is the other reason that wall panel is kept empty. Second, the rust-dungarees girl standing next to the dusty rose awning is a warm-on-warm pairing; it holds only because the awning is 9px tall, desaturated, and 60px above where she stands. The red cap is fine: the awning rose is far duller and never touches the floor line.
risk
The silhouette is nearly a plain block, and that is the real risk. A corner shopfront is architecturally a box; the only thing distinguishing this outline from a generic dark rectangle is the 12px step where the entrance bay rises above the two wings. Every actual cafe cue - stacked chairs, the pendant, the furled awning rolls, the glazed door - is interior or surface detail, and at 120px against busy wallpaper those flatten into coloured patches. I rendered the mock at true 120px to check: it reads as "small lit shopfront", which is right, but I will not claim it reads specifically as "cafe" at a glance. It could be misread as a kiosk, a phone box, or a vending machine, and the brief says silhouette does the work. The mock cannot show the two things I am relying on to rescue that - the soft-painted falloff inside the window glow, and the mist on the parapet - so judge the mock's shape harshly and assume the finished art only gains atmosphere, not outline. Second risk, smaller: the night state deliberately drops to two warm points, and on a dark desktop wallpaper it may recede too far. The mitigation is baked in (the threshold seam never dims below #B4854F and the pavement stays a step lighter than the wall), but if it still vanishes, the fix is to lift the whole night frame's value rather than to add a second lamp - a second lamp kills the "winding down" story.

The comfortable ache of walking home past a shop that should be shut, seeing one bulb still on, and knowing whoever is in there is in no hurry at all.

the cast
It frames them, and it was built to. Every large area is cool and desaturated — slate blue wall, muted teal boards, violet shade — which are the near-complements of cream, apricot, red, rust, orange and gold, so the characters pop forward without the building shouting. Second, the cast is soft-painted and brighter in value than this building will ever be, so the two styles are relatives, not twins — the house has to read as the quiet set behind the actors, and if someone later asks for the house to be "brighter and friendlier" the whole direction collapses.
risk
The real risk is that this is a generic little shop. A centred door with one bay either side, a brim and a stepped parapet is a silhouette the eye files as "small building" in a quarter second; nothing in it says records except the crate, and a crate of sleeves at 120px is a violet box with a pale stripe on top. All the identity lives in the lighting, not the subject, so the direction only works if the generated art keeps soft gradient light — an amber falloff on the boards, a sheen on the glass. If the generator returns flat blocks of colour with a hard outline, you get a teal box with a yellow square, and the mock is honest about that because the mock IS flat: it cannot show the bulb's cone under the awning, the rain in the air, or the warm-to-cool fade down the shopfront, which is most of the feeling. Second risk, structural: the pet covers roughly x24-72, y48-112 whenever it is home, which swallows the left bay and the whole door, so only the right bay, the crate and the fascia are ever reliably visible — I have loaded those with the light on purpose, but it means half the artwork is paid for and hidden. Third, smaller: the crate protrudes past the right wall to break the rectangle, and if the generator draws it detached by even one pixel the frame-count check fails.

Three risks, most likely first, and the first one is a build failure rather than a taste problem. (1) STATE DRIFT. I read ~/workspace/scratch/assets-run/tools/slice-house.js: it crops each of the three states to its own connected-component bounding box and then bottom-centres them in a shared canvas. If the night plume is taller, or a lantern glow reaches a pixel further than in the closed state, the whole building shifts inside the frame and the doorway stops lining up with where the code stands the pet. Steam is exactly the element most likely to drift between frames. The design rule is therefore absolute: identical extents in all three states, colour changes only. (2) IT READS AS AN AWNING, NOT A HOME. A wide horizontal canopy is a strong silhouette but it says "market stall, open for business", not "bedtime".
